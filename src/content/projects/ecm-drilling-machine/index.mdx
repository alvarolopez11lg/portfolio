## What is ECM?

Electrochemical machining (ECM) is a non-traditional process that removes metal by controlled anodic dissolution rather than by mechanical cutting. The workpiece is connected as the anode (+) and a shaped tool electrode as the cathode (−). A low-voltage, high-current DC supply drives current across a narrow gap filled with a flowing electrolytic solution, and the geometry of the tool is progressively reproduced in the workpiece.

The electrolytic solution is the medium in which the process takes place, not only a coolant. In a sodium chloride (NaCl) solution, dissociated ions carry the current across the gap. At the anode, metal atoms release electrons and enter the solution as ions. At the cathode, water is reduced and hydrogen gas is released, so the tool itself is not consumed. The dissolved metal then combines with hydroxide ions to form an insoluble hydroxide sludge, which the electrolyte flow removes from the gap together with the gas and heat. For an aluminum workpiece:

<Equation
  label="Electrode reactions (aluminum workpiece, NaCl electrolyte)"
  tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \text{Anode:}\quad & \mathrm{Al} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al^{3+}} + 3e^- \\ \text{Cathode:}\quad & \mathrm{2H_2O} + 2e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{2OH^-} \\ \text{In solution:}\quad & \mathrm{Al^{3+}} + \mathrm{3OH^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al(OH)_3} \end{aligned}`}
/>

Because metal is removed atom by atom, the removal rate is governed by the electrical charge passed (Faraday's law), not by the hardness, strength or toughness of the material. Typical operating conditions are 8 to 30 V, current densities of 10 to 100 A/cm², a machining gap of about 0.1 mm and tool feed rates of 0.1 to 20 mm/min. Since there is no contact and no cutting force, the machined surface is free of burrs, residual stress and heat-affected zones. This makes ECM well suited to difficult-to-machine alloys such as titanium, nickel superalloys and tungsten carbide.

Industrial applications include turbine and compressor blades, die sinking, deburring of gears and fuel-system components, electrochemical broaching, and drilling of complex or offset holes. The principal limitations are the difficulty of holding tight tolerances, the corrosive electrolyte, sludge disposal and a high specific energy of 200 to 600 J/mm³.

## Goal

Design and build a single-axis electrochemical machine that drills holes in aluminum: a 1/8" hole through 1/8" aluminum 6061, built safely around the electrolytic solution, hydrogen gas and a DC power supply. We're a team of three, and I lead the mechanical design.

## Solution

- **Structure:** a 5-gallon bucket is the frame, reservoir and splash enclosure. 3D-printed brackets hold the Z axis and a slotted workpiece platform.
- **Motion:** an aluminum Z-axis kit with a NEMA 17 stepper and a 4 mm lead screw, homed on limit switches. An ESP32 drives a constant, adjustable feed.
- **Tool:** a 1/8" copper tube electrode.
- **Electrolyte:** a 12 V submersible pump pushes the electrolytic solution through a flow-control valve to a 3D-printed nozzle fixed at the workpiece. The solution drains through the platform back into the reservoir.
- **Safety:** an E-stop cuts mains power to both supplies, every 12 V branch is fused, and a 12 V fan vents hydrogen from under the lid.

## Sizing

<Equation
  label="Material removal rate per amp (Faraday's law, Al 6061)"
  tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned} K_v &= \frac{A}{n\,F\,\rho} \\ &= \frac{26.98}{3 \times 96{,}485 \times 0.0027} \approx 2.07\ \frac{\text{mm}^3}{\text{A}\cdot\text{min}} \end{aligned}`}
  where="A = atomic mass (g/mol), n = valence, F = Faraday constant (C/mol), ρ = density (g/mm³)"
/>

<Equation
  label="Cutting current (34 mm³ hole in 10 min at 60% efficiency)"
  tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned} I &= \frac{V/t}{\eta\,K_v} \\ &= \frac{34/10}{0.6 \times 2.07} \approx 2.7\ \text{A} \end{aligned}`}
  where="V = volume removed, t = cut time, η = current efficiency"
/>

That sets the first wet runs at 8 to 12 V with a 3.5 A current limit, an over-current trip at about 1.5 times the cutting current, and a 6 A inline fuse, all on a 0 to 30 V, 10 A bench supply.
